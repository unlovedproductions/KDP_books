## 2. CHAPTER-OPENING QUOTE VERIFICATION

MDS rule: max one short opening quote per chapter; omit if unverified. All eight Book 1 slots have a clean verified fit.

### Ch 1 — Practice vs discourse  
**Theme:** method ≠ quotes / theorems  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 46, trans. George Long (1890) `[S10]`

**Verified text (use this excerpt):**

> On no occasion call yourself a philosopher, and do not speak much among the uninstructed about theorems (philosophical rules, precepts): but do that which follows from them. For example at a banquet do not say how a man ought to eat, but eat as you ought to eat.

**Shorter printable option (same chapter, same translator):**

> Do you also show not your theorems to the uninstructed, but show the acts which come from their digestion.

**Verification pathway:** Perseus Long *Enchiridion* ch. 46; Wikisource Long *Encheiridion* §XLVI.

**Do not use:** viral “don’t explain your philosophy, embody it” posters without this citation.

### Ch 2 — Impressions  
**Theme:** appearance / *phantasia*  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1, trans. George Long (1890) `[S3]` / `[S1]`

**Verified text:**

> Straightway then practise saying to every harsh appearance, You are an appearance, and in no manner what you appear to be.

**Optional continuation (same chapter) if layout allows one sentence more:**

> Then examine it by the rules which you possess, and by this first and chiefly, whether it relates to the things which are in our power or to things which are not in our power: and if it relates to any thing which is not in our power, be ready to say, that it does not concern you.

**Verification pathway:** Perseus Long *Enchiridion* ch. 1; Wikisource Long §I.

### Ch 4 — Dichotomy of control  
**Theme:** what is up to us  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1, trans. George Long (1890) `[S1]`

**Verified text:**

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion (ὑπόληψις), movement towards a thing (ὁρμή), desire, aversion (ἔκκλισις, turning from a thing); and in a word, whatever are our own acts: not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts.

**Shorter printable option:**

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not.

**Critical label:** Control Sort’s third column (“my share”) is a **publishing teaching aid**, not a third ontological category in Epictetus.

### Ch 5 — Assent / use of impressions  
**Theme:** pause before agreeing  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.18, trans. George Long (1890) `[S11]`

**Verified text:**

> But in the first place be not hurried away by the rapidity of the appearance, but say, Appearances, wait for me a little: let me see who you are, and what you are about: let me put you to the test.

**Verification pathway:** Perseus Long *Discourses* 2.18 (“How we should struggle against appearances”).

**Do not use** Oldfather (“Wait for me a little, O impression…”), Carter, Matheson, or Hard in this block.

**Optional glossary line (not a second epigraph):** *Ench.* 6 Long: “What then is your own? The use of appearances.” `[S14]`

**Integrity note:** Assent Pause as a boxed series drill is **publishing method**. The ancient point is examining appearances before being carried off.

### Ch 6 — Ownership without self-abuse  
**Theme:** responsibility / self-correction without rage at self  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5, trans. George Long (1890) `[S2]` — different excerpt from Ch 3, same verified chapter (same pattern as Book 2 using *De Ira* 3.36 twice).

**Verified text:**

> It is the act of an ill-instructed man to blame others for his own bad condition; it is the act of one who has begun to be instructed, to lay the blame on himself; and of one whose instruction is completed, neither to blame another, nor himself.

**Optional alt (if layout must avoid repeating *Ench.* 5):** Seneca, *De Ira* 3.36, Stewart (1889): “I pardon you this time: see that you never do that anymore?” `[S7]` — self-correction, not self-prosecution. Prefer *Ench.* 5 as the doctrinal fit for “completed instruction.”

### Ch 7 — Virtue as action  
**Theme:** virtue in activity, not labels  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 9.16, trans. George Long `[S5]`

**Verified text:**

> Not in passivity, but in activity lie the evil and the good of the rational social animal, just as his virtue and his vice lie not in passivity, but in activity.

**Verification pathway:** Lexundria Long Book 9 §16.

**Do not use** Haines 1916 (“Not in being acted upon…”) or Farquharson (“Not in feeling but in action…”) in this block.

**Label:** “four virtues as action tests” is series pedagogy. Cardinal-virtue names (wisdom/prudence, justice, courage, temperance/moderation) rest on the standard Stoic taxonomy `[S6]`, not on this Marcus sentence alone.

### Ch 8 — Evening examination  
**Theme:** daily self-scrutiny  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Seneca, *De Ira* (*Of Anger*) 3.36, trans. Aubrey Stewart (1889) `[S7]`

**Verified text:**

> It was the custom of Sextius when the day was over, and he had betaken himself to rest, to inquire of his spirit: “What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?”

**Optional second excerpt (same chapter; use in lesson, not as a second epigraph unless MDS allows):**

> I pass the whole day in review before myself, and repeat all that I have said and done: I conceal nothing from myself, and omit nothing.

**Verification pathway:** Wikisource *Of Anger/Book III* §XXXVI; Gutenberg #64576 (Stewart 1889).

**Label:** series Evening Examination prompts/form = publishing methodology. Seneca supplies the tradition, not the Collection worksheet.
