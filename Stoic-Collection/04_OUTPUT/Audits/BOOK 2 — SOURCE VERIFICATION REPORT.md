## 2. CHAPTER-OPENING QUOTE VERIFICATION

MDS rule: max one short opening quote per chapter; **omit if unverified**.

### Ch 1 — The Pattern You Prefer Not to Name  
**Theme:** self-examination  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Seneca, *De Ira* (*Of Anger*) 3.36, trans. Aubrey Stewart (1889)  

**Verified text (use this excerpt):**

> It was the custom of Sextius when the day was over, and he had betaken himself to rest, to inquire of his spirit: “What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?”

**Verification pathway:**  
- Latin locus: *De Ira* 3.36 (Sextius / daily examination) — standard scholarly locus for Stoic evening self-scrutiny  
- English: Wikisource *Of Anger/Book III* §XXXVI; Gutenberg *Minor Dialogues* (Stewart 1889)  
- Cross-check: Italian/Latin teaching editions confirm same Latin questions (*quod hodie malum tuum sanasti…*)

**Do not use:** Internet “Seneca evening review” paraphrases without this citation.  
**Note:** Seneca *Ep.* 83 discusses reviewing/disclosing the day; it is **related but secondary**. Prefer *De Ira* 3.36 for the examination ritual.

### Ch 6 — What Is Yours to Own  
**Theme:** dichotomy of control  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1, trans. George Long (1890)  

**Verified text:**

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion (ὑπόληψις), movement towards a thing (ὁρμή), desire, aversion (ἔκκλισις, turning from a thing); and in a word, whatever are our own acts: not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts.

**Shorter printable option (same source):**

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not.

**Verification pathway:** Perseus Long; Wikisource; Greek opening τῶν ὄντων τὰ μέν ἐστιν ἐφ’ ἡμῖν… (jtauber / DCC-compatible)

**Critical label for manuscript:** The series tool **Control Sort** third column (“my share”) is a **publishing teaching aid**, not a third ontological category in Epictetus. Must be labeled as such in lesson + Terminology Guide.

### Ch 7 — What You’re Chasing and Fleeing  
**Theme:** desire / aversion  
**Status:** ✅ APPROVED  

**Citation:** Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 2, trans. Elizabeth Carter (1758) *[preferred for clarity of “Remove aversion…”]*  
Alt acceptable: George Long wording of same chapter.

**Verified text (Carter):**

> Remove Aversion, then, from all Things that are not in our Power, and transfer it to Things contrary to the Nature of what is in our Power. But, for the present, totally suppress Desire: for, if you desire any of the Things not in our own Power, you must necessarily be disappointed…

**Verification pathway:**  
- Wikisource Carter *Encheiridion*  
- Sacred-texts / Mises reprints of Carter tradition  
- Greek locus: *Ench.* 2  

**Manuscript caution:** “Totally suppress Desire” is **beginner training language in Epictetus**, not a command to erase all human wanting forever. Lesson must explain this as training of desire/aversion toward what is up to us — and that **feeling may remain** while assent is trained (Series Bible boundary).

If using Long instead of Carter, quote Long’s *Ench.* 2 full corresponding sentences and attribute Long — do not blend Carter+Long in one block.
