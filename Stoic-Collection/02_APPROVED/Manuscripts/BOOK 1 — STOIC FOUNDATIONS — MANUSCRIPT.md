# INTRODUCTION — The Work Begins Here
## Pages 1–5

## Page 1 — What this book is

This book is a finishable program that installs a usable Stoic method.

Its job is practical: take you from slogan familiarity to running a repeatable loop on ordinary life incidents — the late message, the traffic, the short reply, the meeting that ran long.

You will not collect inspirational maxims. You will work one week of real scenes through:

**impression → judgment → control → assent → review**

Stoic philosophy supplies the minimum doctrine required to run that loop: impressions, opinions, the dichotomy of control, assent, virtue as a test of the next act, and a tradition of evening self-examination.[S1][S2][S5][S7][S12]

By the last page you should be able to say, honestly: *I can see the impression, sort what is mine, pause before I agree, own my share, choose one act, and review the day — without another book in my hand.*

The gym is ordinary life. Not catastrophe. Not a hunt for your “real wound.” A scene from this week is enough.

# CHAPTER 2 — What Hits the Mind
## Pages 16–25 · Band I · SEE

## Page 16 — Opening

**Chapter 2**  
# What Hits the Mind

**After this chapter you can:** capture an impression as it strikes — before you treat it as a finished judgment.

> Straightway then practise saying to every harsh appearance, You are an appearance, and in no manner what you appear to be.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1, trans. George Long (1890) [S3]

---

## Pages 17–18 — Lesson

An **impression** (*phantasia*) is what strikes the mind before you decide it is true: a thought, an image, a bodily jolt, a “take” that appears.[S3][S12]

It is not yet belief. Belief requires **assent** — agreement — which you will train to delay in Chapter 5.[S12] First you have to see the strike.

Epictetus trains a first move: treat a harsh appearance as an appearance, not as the thing it claims to be.[S3] You do not have to become numb. You have to stop confusing the first hit with a verdict.

Ordinary examples (not catastrophes):

- The typing-dots in a message, then silence  
- Someone’s tone in a two-line email  
- A cup left in the sink  
- Being interrupted in a meeting  

The impression might be: *I’m being dismissed.* That may or may not be true. Chapter 2’s job is to catch the appearance. Chapter 3 splits what you added.

**Classical vs publishing:** The doctrine of impressions is classical. **Incident Capture** is a publishing worksheet for catching them.

## Pages 26–35 · Band I→II · SORT (judgment)

## Page 26 — Opening

**Chapter 3**  
# Facts and Additions

**After this chapter you can:** split an ordinary incident into facts and added judgments — without turning the page into a character trial.

> Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5, trans. George Long (1890) [S2]

---

## Pages 27–28 — Lesson

A **judgment** (opinion) is an evaluative belief about what happened — not “being judgmental” as a social insult.[S2]

Epictetus’s point is blunt: disturbance follows opinions about events more than the events themselves.[S2] The meeting running long is a fact. *They don’t respect me* is an addition until you have evidence and until you have assented.

You will **pause assent** as a full skill in Chapter 5. Here you only learn to see the seam: camera-facts vs the evaluation you glued on.

Worked T-chart (ordinary scene, composite):

| Facts | Added judgments |
| --- | --- |
| The message arrived at 4:12. It contained one word: “k.” | They’re furious. They’re done with me. I ruined it. |
| I sent my question at 3:40. | I was too much. |

The left column can be checked. The right column may be true, false, or mixed — but it is not a fact until tested, and it is not yet a belief you must live inside.

**This is not Book 2.** You are not hunting a hidden pattern or an avoided truth. You are splitting one ordinary scene so later tools have clean material.

# CHAPTER 4 — Up to You
## Pages 36–45 · Band II · SORT (control)

## Page 36 — Opening

**Chapter 4**  
# Up to You

**After this chapter you can:** run Control Sort on an ordinary incident — three columns — without pretending you control everything or nothing.

> Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion (ὑπόληψις), movement towards a thing (ὁρμή), desire, aversion (ἔκκλισις, turning from a thing); and in a word, whatever are our own acts: not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1, trans. George Long (1890) [S1]

---

## Pages 37–38 — Lesson

The **dichotomy of control** is classical: some things are in our power; others are not.[S1] In our power: opinion, impulse, desire, aversion, and our own acts. Not in our power: body, property, reputation, office, and whatever is not our own act.[S1]

Two folklore errors to kill here:

1. **“I control everything if I try harder.”** You do not control other people’s replies, the clock, or your reputation. Fighting the unmovable is not discipline. It is a category error.
2. **“Nothing is up to me, so I am off the hook.”** Your opinions and acts remain yours. Nihilism is not Stoicism.

**Control Sort** is a publishing worksheet with three columns: *Up to me* | *Not up to me* | *My share*.

**Label this clearly:** “My share” is a **teaching aid** for mixed cases (you cannot control the delay; you can control the follow-up you send). It is **not** a third ontological category in Epictetus.[S1] Classical teaching remains two-sided. The third column exists so you do not dump a mixed scene into “not mine” and walk away, and so you do not swallow the whole scene as “all mine.”

On a first pass, “my share” may be thin. That is allowed. Chapter 6 will require it to be non-empty.

# CHAPTER 5 — Before You Assent
## Pages 46–55 · Band II · Pause

## Page 46 — Opening

**Chapter 5**  
# Before You Assent

**After this chapter you can:** run Assent Pause on a live ordinary trigger — Impression → Pause → Remainder.

> But in the first place be not hurried away by the rapidity of the appearance, but say, Appearances, wait for me a little: let me see who you are, and what you are about: let me put you to the test.
>
> — Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.18, trans. George Long (1890) [S11]

---

## Pages 47–48 — Lesson

**Assent** (*sunkatathesis*) is the act of agreeing with an impression so it becomes a believed judgment.[S12] Humans can examine appearances as true or false; that capacity is what makes the pause possible.[S12]

Epictetus trains not being swept off by the speed of the appearance: wait, see what it is, put it to the test.[S11] **Assent Pause** is the series box for that training. The box is publishing-created. The doctrine is not.

Why we agree too fast: **desire** and **aversion** — the impulse toward what we want and away from what we dislike — make some appearances feel urgent.[S4] Beginners are taught to relocate aversion and to restrain desire for what is not in their power.[S4] That is not a command to erase all human wanting. Feeling may remain while you delay the *agreement*.[S12]

**Do not turn this chapter into a driver map.** You are not cataloging what you chase and flee as Book 2 will. You are answering one question: *Did I agree with the first take before I tested it?*

What is your own, in Epictetus’s line, includes the **use of appearances**.[S14] The pause is how you use them instead of being used by them.

# CHAPTER 6 — Ownership Without Abuse
## Pages 56–65 · Band II · OWN

## Page 56 — Opening

**Chapter 6**  
# Ownership Without Abuse

**After this chapter you can:** name a non-empty “my share” on a new ordinary incident — and drop what was never yours — without turning ownership into self-abuse.

> It is the act of an ill-instructed man to blame others for his own bad condition; it is the act of one who has begun to be instructed, to lay the blame on himself; and of one whose instruction is completed, neither to blame another, nor himself.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5, trans. George Long (1890) [S2]

---

## Pages 57–58 — Lesson

Epictetus maps three stages: blame others; then blame yourself; then, when instruction is completed, blame neither.[S2] This book is not claiming you are “complete.” It is claiming that **accurate ownership** is not a war on the self.

**Own:** the opinion you assented to; the act you chose; the follow-up that was yours.[S1][S2]  
**Do not own:** other people’s minds, the past as if you could rewind it, a total verdict on your worth.

Self-prosecution (“I am the problem, always”) is the beginner move of blaming oneself as a new identity. It is not the completed move. Evening Examination (Chapter 8) will **correct**; it will not indict.

Reuse Control Sort here on a **new** ordinary incident. Column three must not be empty. If you cannot find a share, you are still dumping or still swallowing. Look again: one opinion, one act, one sentence you will send, one pause you skipped.

“Responsibility,” as this series uses it, is accurate attribution of assent and action. That emphasis is ours. The dichotomy underneath it is classical.[S1]

## Pages 67–68 — Lesson

Marcus locates good and evil — and virtue and vice — in **activity**, not passivity.[S5] A well-worded page that never becomes an act is incomplete for this book’s purpose.

# CHAPTER 8 — Review Until It Is Ordinary

## Page 76 — Opening

**Chapter 8**  
# Review Until It Is Ordinary

**After this chapter you can:** run the series Evening Examination on today’s ordinary incidents and extract one correction for tomorrow.

> It was the custom of Sextius when the day was over, and he had betaken himself to rest, to inquire of his spirit: “What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?”
>

---

## Pages 77–78 — Lesson

Seneca describes a nightly practice: the spirit is examined; Sextius asks what was cured, checked, and improved; Seneca reviews the day’s words and deeds without concealment, then sleeps.[S7]

That is the **classical self-examination tradition**. It is not a command to prosecute yourself. Correction follows the review. Concealment is the failure.

**Evening Examination** in this Collection is a **publishing form** — three questions aimed at method literacy. Inspired by the tradition. Not identical with Seneca’s wording. Do not tell anyone “the Stoics invented this worksheet.”

**Series questions (lock this function):**

1. What impression did I notice, and what did I add?  
2. What was up to me, and did I own my share?  
3. What one act will I choose tomorrow?  

Question 3 is a method/practice focus: one act, not a personality overhaul.

You already closed Chapters 1–7 with a Progress Mark so this form would not become a slogan before you had incidents. Tonight it becomes a skill.

Optional classical texture (same chapter, not a second epigraph):

> I pass the whole day in review before myself, and repeat all that I have said and done: I conceal nothing from myself, and omit nothing.
>
> — Seneca, *De Ira* 3.36, trans. Aubrey Stewart (1889) [S7]

Use that as a standard of honesty, not as a license to self-attack.

## Pages 95–99 — Entries

### Impression (*phantasia*)
**Reader definition:** What strikes the mind before you decide it is true — a thought, image, bodily jolt, or “take” that appears.  
**Classical:** The appearance presented to the mind; Epictetus trains treating harsh appearances as appearances first.[S3][S12]  
**In this book:** Incident Capture in Chapter 2.  
**Citation:** [S1], [S3], [S12]

### Assent (*sunkatathesis*)
**Reader definition:** The act of agreeing with an impression so it becomes a believed judgment.  
**Classical:** Faculty of examining impressions as true or false; humans can wait and test appearances.[S12][S11]  
**In this book:** What Assent Pause interrupts. The Pause **box** is a series method.  
**Citation:** [S11], [S12], [S14]

### Judgment / opinion
**Reader definition:** An evaluative belief about what happened — not merely “being judgmental” as a social insult.  
**Classical:** Opinions about things shape disturbance.[S2]  
**In this book:** The right column of the Fact/Judgment Split.  
**Citation:** [S2]

### Dichotomy of control
**Reader definition:** Some things are in our power; others are not.  
**Classical:** *Enchiridion* 1 — opinion, impulse, desire, aversion, and our acts vs body, property, reputation, office, etc.[S1]  
**In this book:** Foundation of Control Sort. The “my share” column is a **teaching aid** for mixed cases, not a third classical category.  
**Citation:** [S1]

### Desire / Aversion
**Reader definition:** The impulse toward what you want and away from what you fear or dislike.  
**Classical:** Train these faculties; beginners are told to restrain desire for what is not in their power and to relocate aversion.[S4]  
**In this book:** Why assent is fast (Chapter 5) — not a full driver map. Feeling may remain while you delay agreement.[S12]  
**Citation:** [S4]

### Virtue
**Reader definition:** Excellence of rational agency expressed in action; for Stoics, the moral good.  
**Classical:** Virtue and vice show in activity, not passivity.[S5] Four cardinal types: wisdom, justice, courage, temperance/moderation.[S6][S13]  
**In this book:** Tests for the next act (light Virtue Mirror). “Action tests” is series pedagogy.  
**Citation:** [S5], [S6], [S13]

### Wisdom
**Reader definition:** Sound judgment about what is good, bad, and indifferent in a situation.  
**Classical:** One of the four cardinal virtues.[S6][S13]  
**In this book:** Virtue Mirror — “What is actually going on?”  
**Citation:** [S5], [S6]

### Courage
**Reader definition:** Right endurance and daring for the sake of virtue — including ordinary difficulty, not only heroics.  
**Classical:** Cardinal virtue; not recklessness.[S6][S13]  
**In this book:** Starting the next act you would postpone.  
**Citation:** [S5], [S6]

### Justice
**Reader definition:** Giving what is owed in dealings and roles — without martyrdom or false guilt.  
**Classical:** Cardinal social virtue.[S6][S13]  
**In this book:** What the next act owes others and your roles.  
**Citation:** [S5], [S6]

### Temperance
**Reader definition:** Self-mastery regarding desire and impulse; measured response rather than indulgence or harsh self-punishment.  
**Classical:** Cardinal virtue tied to training desire/aversion.[S4][S6]  
**In this book:** Pause + measured act; no self-prosecution.  
**Citation:** [S4], [S5], [S6]

### Character
**Reader definition:** The settled disposition built by repeated judgments and acts.  
**Classical-compatible:** Shown in activity.[S5]  
**In this book:** Draft Personal Standard Card — not a personality quiz and not a Character Audit.  
**Citation:** [S5]

### Discipline
**Reader definition:** Trained choice — repeating the loop until it is available under ordinary friction.  
**Note:** Series interpretation, not a single ancient slogan line.  
**In this book:** Practice Commitments with evidence.

### Responsibility (as used here)
**Reader definition:** Accurate ownership of your judgments and acts — without false guilt for what was never yours.  
**Note:** Emphasis is series interpretation built on the dichotomy of control and *Ench.* 5’s completed instruction.[S1][S2]

### Self-examination / evening review tradition
**Reader definition:** A daily practice of reviewing acts and faults without concealment.  
**Classical:** Seneca describes daily examination, including Sextius’s questions and his own review of words and deeds.[S7]  
**In this book:** Evening Examination (series three-question form).  
**Citation:** [S7]

### SEE → SORT → OWN → CHOOSE → REVIEW (SSOCR)
**Reader definition:** The Collection’s portable loop.  
**Classification:** **Publishing-created.** Not a classical technical term.

### Control Sort / Assent Pause / Evening Examination
**Reader definition:** Series worksheets for dichotomy, delayed agreement, and nightly correction.  
**Classification:** **Publishing-created.** “My share” is a teaching aid. Evening Examination wording is series form.
